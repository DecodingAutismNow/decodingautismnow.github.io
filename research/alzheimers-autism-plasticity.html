Abstract
Background

Alzheimer’s disease and autism spectrum disorder are conventionally treated as unrelated conditions separated by the full arc of the human lifespan. This article proposes that both represent expressions of the same underlying failure in synaptic plasticity regulation, driven by a shared mechanistic cascade: chronic immune activation, microglial priming — marked by the inflammatory protein galectin-3 — IDO1-mediated kynurenine pathway dysregulation, quinolinic acid excess, NMDA receptor over-stimulation, and consequent suppression of somatostatin interneuron function.

Key Argument

In Alzheimer’s, this cascade is triggered in late life by reactivating latent pathogens — principally HSV-1, cytomegalovirus, and Porphyromonas gingivalis — and results in progressive destruction of established synaptic architecture. In autism, the same cascade is triggered prenatally or in early childhood by maternal infection or immune activation and disrupts the calibration of a nervous system still under construction. The direction of plasticity failure differs; the machinery is identical.

Significance

This convergence is not merely theoretical. The same inflammatory protein — galectin-3 — is elevated in Alzheimer’s brain tissue and in individuals with autism (serum and animal models). Drugs originally developed for Alzheimer’s are now being tested in autism, with mixed results to date. The convergence reframes both conditions as neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative expressions of a single immune-metabolic failure mode, with shared therapeutic targets at opposite ends of a lifespan.

Section 01

The Mystery That Wasn’t

Picture two patients. The first is an 80-year-old woman who spent decades as a sharp, engaged professional. Over the past few years her family has watched her drift — losing words, losing memories, losing herself. Her neurologist finds amyloid plaques and tau tangles and delivers the diagnosis: Alzheimer’s disease.

The second is a three-year-old boy. He spoke his first words on schedule. Then, somewhere around his second birthday, the words stopped coming. He no longer responds to his name. He moves through the house in repetitive loops, unreachable in ways his parents struggle to explain. His developmental paediatrician delivers the diagnosis: autism spectrum disorder.

Two diagnoses. Two entirely different phases of life. Two conditions that medical textbooks have never placed in the same chapter.

And yet. Buried in the molecular biology of both conditions — in the firing patterns of immune cells, in the chemistry of a single amino acid pathway, in the architecture of synapses that either fail to form properly or fail to hold — lies a story only now becoming visible. Alzheimer’s and autism are not mirror images of each other. But they are, in a very precise sense, expressions of the same underlying failure. The same machinery. The same cascade and pathways, the same mechanisms — at opposite ends of a lifespan.

“We are not looking at two diseases. We are looking at one failure mode, expressed at opposite ends of a life.”
Section 02

The Brain’s Renovation System

To understand what goes wrong in both conditions, you first need to understand what the healthy brain is trying to do. The brain is not a static structure. It is constantly remodelling itself — strengthening connections that are used, pruning away connections that are not, adjusting the sensitivity of receiving neurons to match the signals coming in. This process, synaptic plasticity, is the biological basis of learning, memory, and development.

The cells primarily responsible for this renovation work are microglia — the brain’s resident immune cells. Microglia survey the neural landscape continuously, tagging weak or damaged synapses for removal, clearing cellular debris, and maintaining the chemical environment that allows healthy neurons to communicate. When the brain is working well, microglia are precise, regulated, and responsive. They renovate when renovation is needed. They stand down when the work is done.

One molecular system closely linked to when microglia activate and when they stand down is the kynurenine pathway — a metabolic cascade that begins with the amino acid tryptophan and, under inflammatory conditions, produces a neurotoxic compound called quinolinic acid (QUIN).10 QUIN is a potent activator of NMDA receptors, the ligand-gated, voltage-dependent channels that control calcium entry into neurons and regulate the strength of synaptic connections.

In a healthy brain, QUIN is produced in tiny, tightly controlled amounts. In a brain under chronic immune activation, IDO1 — the enzyme that opens the kynurenine pathway — is switched on continuously, flooding neural tissue with QUIN, over-exciting NMDA receptors, and creating sustained excitotoxicity that damages and destroys synapses. A key marker of this activated microglial state is the protein galectin-3 — a carbohydrate-binding lectin upregulated when microglia shift into their pro-inflammatory, neurotoxic phenotype. Its presence signals that the renovation system has been locked in hyperactivation and cannot stand down.

This is the shared machinery. It operates in both Alzheimer’s disease and autism spectrum disorder. What differs is the trigger, the developmental timing, and the direction of the damage.

Section 03

Alzheimer’s: When Infection Closes Plasticity Down

For decades, the dominant theory of Alzheimer’s has been structural: amyloid plaques accumulate, tau protein tangles, neurons die. The assumption was that plaques were the problem — clear them, and you stop the disease. After decades of disappointing results from amyloid-targeting clinical trials, researchers were forced to ask a harder question: what causes the plaques in the first place?

The answer emerging from the literature is, at its core, an infection story.1 Amyloid-beta (Aβ), long considered purely pathological, has been reframed by researchers at Harvard and elsewhere as an antimicrobial peptide — part of the brain’s innate immune defence.2, 12 When a pathogen breaches the blood-brain barrier, Aβ is deployed to entrap and neutralise it. Under normal circumstances, the immune system clears the threat, the Aβ is reabsorbed, and the episode ends. When the immune system cannot fully clear the pathogen — particularly in an ageing brain whose defences are waning — Aβ keeps being produced. The intended defence becomes the source of damage.

Three pathogens have accumulated the strongest evidence as chronic triggers of this process:

Herpesvirus · HSV-1

Herpes Simplex Virus Type 1

Present as a latent infection in the trigeminal ganglia of approximately two-thirds of adults, HSV-1 periodically reactivates in the ageing brain. Each reactivation triggers microglial activation, promotes Aβ and tau accumulation, and fires the kynurenine pathway. Critically, patients receiving antiviral therapy for HSV-1 were associated with a markedly lower risk of dementia in a large Taiwanese cohort (adjusted HR 0.09) — an observational treatment-response signal that strengthens the case without proving causation.4

Oral Bacterium · P. gingivalis

Porphyromonas gingivalis

The keystone bacterium of chronic gum disease has been detected directly in the brain tissue of Alzheimer’s patients. Its toxic enzymes — gingipains — have been found co-localised with tau tangles and amyloid plaques, with levels correlating with disease severity. Oral infection in mouse models produces brain colonisation, increased Aβ production, and cognitive decline.3, 5

Herpesvirus · CMV

Cytomegalovirus

Carried latently by the majority of adults, CMV was shown in a 2026 study to accelerate cognitive decline, tauopathy and synaptic loss in Alzheimer’s-susceptible (3xTg-AD) mice infected with murine CMV. Treatment with the antiviral valganciclovir during chronic infection reversed cognitive decline — pointing to ongoing viral activity, not only a past infection, as a driver. The mechanism identified in that study was infiltration of virus-specific CD8+ T cells into the brain rather than galectin-3 signalling, so it supports the infection-trigger step of the cascade rather than the downstream microglial steps.6

The mechanism in each case is the same. Chronic, low-grade infection primes microglia into a state of persistent activation — a state marked by elevated galectin-3. Activated microglia drive the kynurenine pathway. QUIN over-stimulates NMDA receptors. Synaptic connections are destabilised, long-term potentiation — the molecular basis of memory formation — is suppressed, much as oligomeric Aβ itself impairs synaptic function.11 Among the downstream casualties are somatostatin (SST-14) interneurons: the cortical inhibitory cells that act as the brain’s signal regulators. Somatostatin-expressing interneuron subtypes are selectively depleted in Alzheimer’s cortex, and lower somatostatin levels correlate with greater cognitive impairment.19, 20 Plasticity closes down. The brain loses its ability to remodel itself, and the architecture of a lifetime of memory begins to dissolve.

Section 04

Autism: When Plasticity Fails to Open

Now hold that mechanism — and wind the clock back 75 years.

The same pathogens that drive Alzheimer’s disease in the ageing brain are, when they act on a developing nervous system, implicated in autism. Congenital CMV infection — the most common congenital infection worldwide, affecting more than 1 in 200 live births — is a recognised risk factor for autism spectrum disorders.7 When CMV crosses the placenta during pregnancy, it colonises neural tissue at precisely the developmental window when the fetal brain’s synaptic architecture is being built. The same microglial priming. The same galectin-3 elevation. The same kynurenine pathway activation. The same QUIN-driven NMDA over-stimulation — but now applied not to a brain that is losing its connections, but to a brain that is still deciding which connections to make.

Beyond specific pathogens, the broader phenomenon of maternal immune activation (MIA) — the fetal brain’s exposure to its mother’s inflammatory state during a vulnerable developmental period — has been established as an environmental risk factor for autism: a 2016 meta-analysis of 15 studies found that maternal infection during pregnancy raised ASD risk (OR 1.13; OR 1.30 when hospitalisation was required).8 The proposed mechanism runs through the kynurenine pathway: in mouse models, maternal immune activation raises kynurenine metabolite levels in the placenta and fetal brain, exposing it to an excitotoxic environment during the window of synaptic formation.9

In the autism case, SST-14 interneurons fail to complete their developmental calibration. Rather than being destroyed, as in Alzheimer’s, they are never properly commissioned. The cortical inhibitory network that SST-14 interneurons form — the network that regulates the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signalling — is miscalibrated from the outset. The result is an excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) ratio that is set incorrectly before the first words are formed. Rather than plasticity closing down, it fails to open properly.

This may explain one of autism’s most clinically puzzling features: the divergence between children who present with disruption from birth versus those who develop apparently normally and then regress. In the congenital infection model, the insult precedes development — the E/I ratio is miscalibrated before the first synapse forms. In the postnatal cascade model, there is a period of normal development before the cascade reaches the brain. Same machinery. Different entry point. Different clinical presentation.

Section 06

The Shared Protein: Galectin-3

Among the most significant recent convergences between Alzheimer’s and autism research is the identification of a single inflammatory protein — galectin-3 — that is elevated in Alzheimer’s brain tissue and in autism (serum and animal models).

Galectin-3 (encoded by the LGALS3 gene) is a carbohydrate-binding lectin expressed primarily by activated microglia. It is not merely a bystander marker: galectin-3 is an active amplifier of its own signal. Once secreted into the extracellular space by activated microglia, it binds TLR2 and TLR4 as an endogenous ligand, triggering sustained NF-κB pro-inflammatory signalling. It simultaneously engages TREM2/DAP12, driving microglial over-phagocytosis of stressed neurons and promoting the cell-to-cell spread of misfolded proteins. It activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, releasing IL-1β and IL-18. It disrupts hippocampal gamma oscillations via integrin-β3 inhibition, and activates GSK-3β, contributing to tau hyperphosphorylation. When galectin-3 is high, the microglial renovation system is locked in a state of chronic, unresolved activation. It cannot stand down.

In Alzheimer’s disease, galectin-3 is markedly upregulated in the brain and is expressed by microglia associated with amyloid plaques; genetic variants in LGALS3 have also been associated with Alzheimer’s risk. In the 5xFAD mouse model, deleting galectin-3 attenuated microglial immune responses (including TLR and TREM2/DAP12 signalling), reduced amyloid burden and improved cognitive behaviour.17 This work provided the rationale for TB006, a humanised monoclonal antibody targeting extracellular galectin-3, developed by TrueBinding Inc. Its Phase 1/2 Alzheimer’s trial (NCT05074498, n=154) reported reduced plasma Aβ42 (the Aβ42/40 ratio was unchanged); its primary outcome, CDR-SB, narrowly missed statistical significance, and the sponsor reported less worsening on that measure versus placebo at three months.18 TrueBinding is now running a Phase 2a Parkinson’s disease trial (NCT06773962, n=62), where galectin-3 mediates α-synuclein-induced microglial inflammation by the same TLR4/NLRP3 pathway — evidence that the mechanism is not disease-specific but cascade-specific.13

The same galectin-3 elevation has been observed in individuals with autism — primarily in serum studies and ASD animal models — consistent with the same state of chronic microglial hyperactivation. This is not a parallel finding in separate biological systems. It is the same protein, elevated for the same mechanistic reason: an immune activation cascade that has been triggered and cannot resolve. A registered Phase 2 RCT of TB006 in autistic adults aged 18–35, sponsored by Rossignol Medical Center, is now recruiting (NCT06500637, n=45, estimated primary completion December 2026; enrolment excludes carriers of two copies of the LGALS3 rs4644 variant) — following the same anti-neuroinflammatory rationale that has guided its Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s development.13, 14

“The same protein is high in the Alzheimer’s brain that triggers inflammation — and it is also elevated in autism, in serum and animal models. That is not a coincidence. That is the same fire.”

Galectin-3’s dual presence in both conditions is a signal that research fields have tended to miss because they look inward at their own condition rather than across the lifespan. The fire in the Alzheimer’s brain and the fire in the autism brain share the same accelerant.

Section 07

Drug Convergence as Evidence

If the shared mechanistic architecture were only theoretical, it would be compelling but inconclusive. What strengthens the argument is the pattern of drug convergence now emerging in clinical trials: medications originally developed for Alzheimer’s disease are being tested in autism, with results so far that are mixed.

This is not random pharmacological experimentation. It is the clinical literature following the inflammation → SST-14 → inhibitory network thread from one end of the lifespan to the other, often without the researchers explicitly naming that thread. The drugs that target the downstream consequences of microglial hyperactivation in Alzheimer’s patients are, in principle, targeting the same downstream consequences in autistic children and adults. The diseases differ in direction; the therapeutic target does not.

AChE Inhibitor

Galantamine

Approved for Alzheimer’s. In autism, one small randomised trial (n=40) of galantamine added to risperidone reported greater improvement in irritability and social withdrawal than placebo; a Cochrane review rates the evidence as low to very low certainty and found little evidence of benefit on core autism features.21, 22 A link to SST-14 interneuron failure is a working hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.

AChE Inhibitor

Donepezil (Aricept)

Alzheimer’s standard of care. In autism, a 34-participant placebo-controlled trial found no significant between-group difference on executive function,23 while a 60-child trial of donepezil plus choline reported improved receptive language six months after treatment, mainly in children under 10.24 Shares the cholinergic rationale with galantamine.

NMDA Antagonist

Memantine (Namenda)

Alzheimer’s NMDA blocker. Tested in autism with mixed results: a 121-child placebo-controlled trial did not show efficacy on its primary outcome, while a smaller add-on-to-risperidone trial was positive.15, 16 The rationale — blocking QUIN-driven NMDA over-stimulation — has not yet been tested in an immune-selected subgroup.

Anti-neuroinflammatory

TB006 (TrueBinding)

Humanised monoclonal antibody targeting extracellular galectin-3, blocking its binding to TLR4, TREM2, and downstream NLRP3/NF-κB signalling. Phase 1/2 Alzheimer’s trials completed (n=154); Phase 2 RCT now actively recruiting in autism spectrum disorder (NCT06500637, Rossignol Medical Center, n=45, estimated primary completion December 2026). TB006 is also in a Phase 2a Parkinson’s disease trial (NCT06773962, n=62, primary completion November 2026) — reinforcing that the galectin-3 neuroinflammation mechanism is pathogen-agnostic and extends across neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental conditions.13

Loop Diuretic / Anti-neuroinflammatory

Bumetanide

Targets GABA signalling maturation. In autism, two large Phase III trials (children 2–6 and 7–17 years; n=211 each) were stopped early because bumetanide did not differ from placebo on the primary outcome or any secondary outcome.25 In Alzheimer’s, a Phase IIa trial (NCT06052163, n=40) is recruiting. The GABA-maturation rationale has not been tested in an immune-selected subgroup.

Rheology / Anti-inflammatory

Pentoxifylline

Phosphodiesterase inhibitor with TNF-α-lowering effects. In autism, one 40-child randomised trial of pentoxifylline added to risperidone reported greater reductions in Aberrant Behavior Checklist scores than placebo,26 and rodent studies report improved autism-like behaviour.27 Evidence is limited to small, single-region trials.

The convergence of Alzheimer’s drugs into autism trials is not a methodological quirk — it is the literature beginning to follow a mechanistic thread that connects the two conditions. Each drug in this list addresses a different node in the same cascade: microglial activation (TB006), NMDA excitotoxicity (memantine), cholinergic disruption downstream of SST-14 loss (galantamine, donepezil), GABA maturation failure (bumetanide). Different entry points. The same fire. Results in autism so far are mixed, and the largest controlled trials (bumetanide Phase III; memantine monotherapy) were negative on their primary outcomes — which may be what one would expect when these agents are tested in unselected populations rather than an immune-derived subgroup.

The cascade does not stop at two conditions. TB006 is currently in a Phase 2a Parkinson’s disease trial (NCT06773962, n=62, primary completion November 2026). In Parkinson’s, galectin-3 mediates α-synuclein-induced microglial inflammation through the same TLR4/NLRP3 pathway described in this article — with dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra as the vulnerable cell population rather than SST-14 interneurons. The trigger differs. The cascade is the same. Read the full four-condition convergence article — autism, Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and multiple sclerosis.

Section 08

Why This Matters Beyond Biology

For Alzheimer’s Research

The infectious hypothesis reframes the entire intervention timeline. If chronic subclinical infection is the upstream trigger — not the amyloid accumulation, which is a downstream consequence — then antiviral and antimicrobial strategies in at-risk individuals (particularly APOE4 carriers, who have impaired pathogen clearance) become the most underexplored prevention lever in neurodegenerative medicine. The markedly lower dementia risk (adjusted HR 0.09) seen with antiviral treatment for HSV-1 in a large observational cohort is not a small signal, though randomised trials are still needed. The reversal of CMV-driven cognitive decline by antiviral treatment in a 2026 mouse model study points in the same direction.4, 6

For Autism Research

The same reframing suggests that the gut-immune-brain cascade driving neuroinflammation in autistic children is not a unique pathology of autism — it is the same immune machinery that, when activated late in life, produces Alzheimer’s disease. This positions autism as a neurodevelopmental expression of a fundamentally immune and metabolic disorder, not primarily a genetic one. Genetics determines susceptibility and severity. The immune cascade determines expression. And galectin-3 is the measurable protein marker that tracks that cascade in both directions.

For Both Fields Together

The shared mechanistic spine — galectin-3 → microglial hyperactivation → kynurenine pathway → QUIN → NMDA excitotoxicity → SST-14 failure → E/I imbalance — is a therapeutic target that both fields are pursuing independently of each other. The drug convergence described in Section 07 is evidence that the fields are, in practice if not in name, beginning to work the same mechanism from both ends of the lifespan. Making that shared target explicit accelerates both research programmes.

“The plasticity framing is precise. One condition fails to open plasticity, or never opens it fully. The other has plasticity closing prematurely, decades before it should. Both are failures of the same renovation system — and galectin-3 is the alarm that is ringing in both.”
Section 09

A Note on Complexity and Humility

This framework should not be mistaken for a complete explanation of either condition. Both autism and Alzheimer’s are heterogeneous. Not every case of Alzheimer’s has a detectable infectious trigger. Not every autistic child has a history of maternal infection or early inflammatory cascade. Genetics, environmental exposures, mitochondrial function, and individual immune response variation all shape how, when, and whether these cascades activate.

What the infectious and immune frameworks provide is not a final answer — but a coherent mechanistic spine onto which the known biology of both conditions can be organised. This is another shade in the spectrum of understanding for both illnesses: a thread that connects two bodies of research that have, until recently, had no shared language. Galectin-3 and SST-14 are nodes in that thread that are now supported by independent lines of evidence in both fields simultaneously.

In fields that have spent decades searching for disease-modifying interventions without a clear causal map to navigate by, a coherent mechanistic spine — even a partial one — is exactly what progress requires.
