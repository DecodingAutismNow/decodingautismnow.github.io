Abstract
Background

Autism spectrum disorder, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and multiple sclerosis are classified as distinct neurological conditions with separate diagnostic criteria, research programmes, and treatment pathways. This article proposes that all four share a common mechanistic backbone: chronic immune activation driving microglial galectin-3 secretion, which sustains neuroinflammation through TLR2/TLR4, TREM2/DAP12, and NLRP3 inflammasome signalling, ultimately causing cell-specific failure in vulnerable neural populations.

Key Finding

What differs across the four conditions is not the cascade — it is the initial trigger, the cell population most vulnerable to that cascade, and the developmental timing of the insult. The same inflammatory protein, galectin-3, is elevated in all four conditions. The same anti-galectin-3 monoclonal antibody, TB006, has now entered registered clinical trials for three of them: completed in Alzheimer’s, active in Parkinson’s and recruiting in autism.7

Significance

Recognising the shared cascade opens a mechanism-first approach to treatment: drugs developed for one condition may have therapeutic relevance across all four. The clinical trial convergence now underway is not coincidence — it is the evidence base beginning to follow a mechanistic thread that diagnostic silos have obscured.

Section 01

The Same Fire, Four Rooms

Medicine has built careful walls between neurological conditions. Autism is classified as a neurodevelopmental disorder, managed by developmental paediatricians and behavioural therapists. Alzheimer’s is a neurodegenerative disease, the domain of geriatric neurologists. Parkinson’s is a movement disorder driven by dopaminergic neuron loss. Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune demyelinating disease. Separate clinics. Separate research programmes. Separate journals. Separate drug pipelines.

The walls are administrative, not biological.

At the cellular and molecular level, all four conditions share a common thread: the brain’s resident immune cells — microglia — have been activated into a chronic, unresolved state. And in all four, the protein that marks and sustains that activation is the same: galectin-3.

Galectin-3 is not merely a bystander elevated in sick brains. It is an active amplifier. Once secreted by activated microglia, it binds TLR2 and TLR4 as an endogenous ligand, triggering sustained NF-κB signalling. It engages TREM2/DAP12, driving microglial over-phagocytosis of stressed neurons and promoting the spread of misfolded proteins. It activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, releasing IL-1β and IL-18. It inhibits integrin-β3 signalling, disrupting hippocampal gamma oscillations. It activates GSK-3β, promoting tau hyperphosphorylation. The fire, once lit, feeds itself.

What differs between the four conditions is not the fire — it is the room it burns in, and when the match was struck.

“The trigger differs. The cell population under attack differs. The timing differs. The cascade is the same.”
Section 02

The Shared Cascade

The common mechanistic backbone across all four conditions can be described in a single sequence. Each condition enters this cascade at different points, with different triggers, but the downstream steps are shared.

The Galectin-3 Neuroinflammation Cascade
Step 1 — TriggerCondition-specific initiating event: prenatal immune activation or congenital pathogen (autism); amyloid-β accumulation or chronic viral infection — HSV-1, CMV, P. gingivalis (Alzheimer’s); α-synuclein misfolding, gut dysbiosis (Parkinson’s); EBV molecular mimicry targeting GlialCAM/myelin (MS)
Step 2 — Microglial Priming + Galectin-3 ElevationPersistent microglial activation; galectin-3 (LGALS3) upregulated and secreted; microglial renovation system locked in hyperactivation; elevated in brain tissue, CSF, and/or serum in all four conditions
Step 3 — TLR4 / TREM2 / NLRP3 AmplificationExtracellular galectin-3 binds TLR2/TLR4 → NF-κB activation; TREM2/DAP12 engagement → phagocytic over-activation and misfolded protein spread; NLRP3 inflammasome → IL-1β and IL-18 release; self-sustaining inflammatory loop
Step 4 — Kynurenine Pathway Activation (neurodegenerative subset)IDO1 enzyme upregulated by chronic immune activation; tryptophan diverted to kynurenine → quinolinic acid (QUIN); NMDA receptor over-stimulation; excitotoxic synapse damage (primary in ASD and AD; also implicated in PD)
Step 5 — Cell-Specific Failure (outcome determined by vulnerability)Autism: SST-14 interneurons fail to commission → E/I imbalance, cortical hyperexcitability  |  Alzheimer’s: SST-14 and cholinergic neurons destroyed → memory loss, cognitive decline  |  Parkinson’s: dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra lost → motor failure  |  MS: oligodendrocytes and myelin sheath attacked → demyelination, signal conduction failure
Step 6 — Clinical ExpressionPhenotype determined by which cells failed and when: neurodevelopmental (autism, early), neurodegenerative (AD, PD, late-life), demyelinating (MS, early adult). The same cascade. Different clocks. Different rooms.

The key insight is that steps 2 and 3 are shared regardless of the trigger in step 1; step 4, the kynurenine arm, is shared most clearly by autism and Alzheimer’s. This is why drugs designed to interrupt the galectin-3 amplification loop — developed for one condition — show plausible therapeutic rationale across all four.

Section 03

Autism: The Developmental Expression

In the Immune-Derived Autism (IDA) framework, autism represents the cascade operating on a developing nervous system — one that has not yet built the synaptic architecture it needs, rather than one that is losing architecture it already built.

Trigger: Prenatal immune activation from maternal infection, congenital pathogens (CMV, HSV-1, rubella), or gut-immune dysregulation in the developing infant. CMV alone — the most common congenital infection globally, affecting >1 in 200 live births — is a recognised risk factor for autism spectrum disorder when it crosses the placenta during the critical fetal neural development window.1

Galectin-3: Elevated in autism serum studies and ASD animal models, consistent with chronic microglial hyperactivation. The same protein elevated in Alzheimer’s brain tissue is elevated in the blood of autistic individuals — for the same mechanistic reason.

Vulnerable cell population: SST-14 (somatostatin-14) interneurons — cortical inhibitory cells that regulate the excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) ratio. When the galectin-3 cascade fires during the developmental window in which these interneurons are being commissioned, they fail to fully develop. The result is cortical hyperexcitability, impaired signal filtering, and the sensory, social, and cognitive profile characteristic of autism spectrum disorder.

Timing: Fetal development through early postnatal life. The cascade does not destroy existing circuitry — it prevents it from forming in the first place.

IDA does not claim that all autism is immune-derived. It proposes that a substantial, identifiable subgroup — characterised by elevated inflammatory markers, GI comorbidity, regression, and immune dysregulation — represents the developmental expression of this cascade. Genetics determines susceptibility and severity. The immune cascade determines expression.

Section 04

Alzheimer’s: The Late-Life Expression

Alzheimer’s disease represents the cascade operating on a fully developed nervous system, decades after construction is complete. The architecture exists and has been in use for a lifetime. The cascade destroys it.

Triggers: Multiple. Amyloid-β accumulation; chronic viral infection — HSV-1 reactivating along neural pathways in the ageing brain, CMV driving accelerated T-cell ageing and cognitive decline,2 Porphyromonas gingivalis (periodontal bacterium) detected in AD brain tissue producing gingipain toxins that drive tau pathology.3

Galectin-3: Markedly upregulated in Alzheimer’s brain and expressed by plaque-associated microglia. Binds TREM2 and, in mice, was required for the formation of long-lasting Aβ aggregates. Galectin-3 deletion in 5xFAD mice attenuated microglial immune responses, reduced amyloid burden and improved cognitive behaviour.9 The dominant theory of Alzheimer’s has been structural (amyloid plaques, tau tangles) — but galectin-3 reframes both as downstream consequences of a primary immune activation failure.4

Vulnerable cell population: SST-14 interneurons (the same cells that fail to commission in autism — here, they are destroyed after decades of function), and cholinergic neurons of the basal forebrain. SST-14 depletion in Alzheimer’s brain tissue correlates directly with the degree of cognitive decline. The cholinergic loss underlies the memory impairment that defines clinical AD and is the target of current first-line treatments.

Timing: Late life, typically after age 65. Decades of cumulative microglial priming, often from multiple overlapping triggers, eventually overwhelm the brain’s anti-inflammatory resolution capacity.

The SST-14 interneuron connection is striking: the same cell type that fails to commission in autism under early-life galectin-3 cascade activation is the same cell type that is destroyed in Alzheimer’s under late-life galectin-3 cascade activation. Same target. Opposite direction of time.

Section 05

Parkinson’s: The Dopaminergic Expression

Parkinson’s disease is characterised by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra — a midbrain structure that controls motor function. The clinical result is tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia (slowness of movement), and postural instability. The trigger and the vulnerable cell population differ from autism and Alzheimer’s, but the galectin-3 neuroinflammation cascade is the same.

Trigger: α-Synuclein misfolding and aggregation into Lewy bodies — a process now understood to begin in the enteric nervous system (gut) and travel to the brain via the vagus nerve. Gut dysbiosis is a recognised feature of Parkinson’s, and the gut-brain axis is increasingly seen as the initiating site of the cascade in a substantial proportion of patients. Environmental toxins (pesticides, heavy metals) and certain infections are also implicated triggers.

Galectin-3: α-Synuclein directly induces microglial galectin-3 secretion. Galectin-3 then mediates the α-synuclein-driven microglial inflammatory response through TLR4 and NLRP3 — the same molecular pathway as in Alzheimer’s and autism. Critically, galectin-3 also promotes secretory autophagy of α-synuclein: activated microglia use galectin-3 to package and release α-synuclein into the extracellular space, spreading the misfolded protein to neighbouring cells. The cascade thus propagates itself through the neural tissue.

Vulnerable cell population: Dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta. These neurons have unusually high energy demands and long, unmyelinated axons — making them particularly vulnerable to oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory damage. Once lost, they do not regenerate. Current treatments (L-DOPA, dopamine agonists) replace the missing dopamine signal but do not halt the underlying neuronal loss.

Timing: Mid-to-late life, typically after age 50–60. The prodromal phase — during which α-synuclein accumulation and gut neuroinflammation have already begun — may precede motor symptoms by 10–20 years.

Clinical trial signal: TB006, the anti-galectin-3 monoclonal antibody, is currently in a Phase 2a Parkinson’s trial (NCT06773962, n=62), with primary completion estimated November 2026. If TB006 shows motor benefit in PD — interrupting the galectin-3/TLR4/NLRP3 cascade — it would constitute direct clinical evidence that the same neuroinflammation mechanism drives neuronal loss across conditions. Results are expected to be posted to ClinicalTrials.gov by late 2027.

The gut-brain connection in Parkinson’s is mechanistically resonant with IDA. In both conditions, gut dysbiosis and immune dysregulation appear to be upstream of brain neuroinflammation — not a symptom of it. The enteric nervous system as the first site of cascade activation is an area of active research in both fields.

Section 06

Multiple Sclerosis: The Myelin Expression

Multiple sclerosis differs from the other three conditions in one important respect: the immune attack is explicitly autoimmune and targets the myelin sheath — the insulating layer around nerve axons — rather than neurons directly. But the galectin-3 neuroinflammation cascade is present here too, sustaining and amplifying the myelin destruction, and the trigger is a virus.

Trigger: EBV and molecular mimicry. In January 2022, Bjornevik et al. published evidence in Science that Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection substantially increases the risk of developing MS — identifying EBV as a probable causal factor rather than an association. The mechanism is molecular mimicry: a portion of the EBV protein EBNA1 structurally resembles GlialCAM, a protein expressed in the myelin sheath. As the immune system generates antibodies to clear EBV, those same antibodies — in genetically susceptible individuals — also bind GlialCAM and attack the myelin it is part of. Elevated anti-EBNA1 / anti-GlialCAM cross-reactive antibodies are found in approximately 20–25% of MS patients across multiple cohorts.5

Galectin-3 in MS: LGALS3 mRNA is significantly elevated in microglia from EAE (experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis) mouse models of MS, and in MS white matter lesions compared with normal white matter. Galectin-3 expression clusters with gene modules linked to microglial activation, cytokine production, and chemotaxis in MS lesion data. Galectin-3-deficient mice develop less severe EAE, and in a small study serum autoantibodies against galectin-3 were found in most secondary progressive MS patients tested, suggesting an additional autoimmune dimension.6

Vulnerable cell population: Oligodendrocytes — the cells that produce and maintain the myelin sheath. Microglial galectin-3 over-activation drives phagocytosis of stressed oligodendrocytes, and galectin-3 overexpression has been shown to reduce oligodendrocyte proportions in gliogenesis models. Loss of myelin disrupts the electrical insulation of axons, slowing or blocking nerve signal conduction. The clinical result is the relapsing-remitting or progressive neurological dysfunction characteristic of MS.

Timing: Early adulthood, typically age 20–40, with higher prevalence in women. The autoimmune trigger is set by EBV infection (most commonly in adolescence or early adulthood as infectious mononucleosis) in individuals with genetic susceptibility at HLA-DRB1 and other immune loci.

The EBV → GlialCAM molecular mimicry pathway is a striking parallel to the viral trigger hypothesis in IDA. In both MS and autism, a viral insult — EBV in MS, CMV or other congenital pathogens in IDA — primes an immune response that then misdirects itself toward neural tissue. The galectin-3 cascade sustains the resulting neuroinflammation in both cases.

Section 07

Drug Convergence as Evidence

If four conditions truly share a mechanistic cascade, the drug pipeline should eventually reflect that — not by design, but by the logic of the evidence. That convergence is now underway. The most direct evidence is TB006; see also the companion article on Alzheimer’s and autism as a plasticity problem.8

Shared Therapeutic Targets

Anti-galectin-3

TB006 (TrueBinding)

Humanised monoclonal antibody targeting extracellular galectin-3. Phase 1/2 Alzheimer’s completed; Phase 2a Parkinson’s active (NCT06773962); Phase 2 autism RCT recruiting (NCT06500637). One anti-galectin-3 antibody now spans trials in three of the four conditions (Alzheimer’s completed; Parkinson’s and autism ongoing).

NMDA antagonist

Memantine

NMDA receptor blocker used in moderate-to-severe Alzheimer’s. Mixed results in autism: a 121-child placebo-controlled trial missed its primary outcome, while a smaller add-on trial to risperidone was positive.10 Rationale: NMDA excitotoxicity downstream of kynurenine pathway activation. Addresses the QUIN-driven over-stimulation that is step 4 of the shared cascade.

Cholinesterase inhibitor

Galantamine / Donepezil

Standard Alzheimer’s treatments targeting cholinergic deficit. In autism the controlled evidence is small and mixed: galantamine added to risperidone improved irritability in one 40-child trial, while a donepezil trial (n=34) found no between-group difference on executive function; a Cochrane review rates the evidence low to very low certainty.11 Any link to SST-14 loss is hypothesised.

NKCC1 antagonist

Bumetanide

Loop diuretic repurposed for GABA maturation failure. Two Phase III autism trials were stopped early with no difference from placebo.12 A Phase IIa trial in Alzheimer’s (NCT06052163, n=40) is recruiting. The GABA-maturation rationale has not been tested in an immune-selected subgroup.

Anti-neuroinflammatory

Pentoxifylline

Phosphodiesterase inhibitor with TNF-α suppression and microcirculation effects. One 40-child randomised trial in autism (added to risperidone) reported greater behavioural improvement than placebo;13 evidence is otherwise preclinical. Targets the downstream cytokine component of NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Anti-viral

Valacyclovir / Valganciclovir

HSV-1 and CMV antivirals. In the VALAD Phase II trial (n=120, HSV-seropositive early Alzheimer’s), valacyclovir was not efficacious and was associated with greater cognitive worsening on the primary outcome than placebo.14 Valganciclovir reversed cognitive decline in 3xTg-AD mice (Marsden et al., Brain 2026). IDA research interest in antiviral strategies for autism subgroups with known congenital viral triggers.

“The same drug has now entered trials for three neurological conditions. That is not a coincidence. That is a mechanism.”
Section 08

Implications: Mechanism-First Medicine

The conventional model of neurological disease is diagnosis-first: identify the condition by its symptoms and clinical presentation, then match it to an approved treatment. The cascade model proposed here inverts that logic. If the same underlying mechanism drives four conditions, then the question is not which condition a patient has — it is where in the cascade that patient’s biology has been disrupted, and when.

Galectin-3 as a universal biomarker. If galectin-3 elevation marks the activated microglial state across all four conditions, it is potentially a shared diagnostic and monitoring tool. A serum galectin-3 level does not diagnose a specific condition — but it may indicate that the shared cascade is active, and that anti-galectin-3 or anti-neuroinflammatory intervention is warranted regardless of the phenotypic label. This is already implicit in the TB006 trial programme, which does not distinguish mechanistically between its AD, PD, and ASD indications — only between the phenotypic populations it is testing in.

Rethinking the diagnostic silos. A child with autism and elevated inflammatory markers, a 70-year-old with early Alzheimer’s and elevated galectin-3, a 55-year-old with Parkinson’s whose disease began in the gut, and a 30-year-old with relapsing-remitting MS triggered by adolescent EBV — these are not four patients with four unrelated diseases. They may be four expressions of the same immune-to-brain failure mode, separated by timing, trigger, and genetic vulnerability profile.

What shared trials would look like. A logical extension of this framework would be a platform trial testing anti-galectin-3 or NLRP3 inhibition across multiple neurological conditions simultaneously, with stratification by baseline galectin-3 level, inflammatory marker profile, and known trigger. The infrastructure for this does not currently exist — but the mechanistic rationale does, and the TB006 trial programme is a de facto approximation of it.

The IDA connection. For Decoding Autism Now, the four-condition framework strengthens the core IDA argument: autism in the immune-derived subgroup is not primarily a genetic disorder with incidental immune features. It is an immune-metabolic disorder with a neurodevelopmental phenotype — one that shares its fundamental biology with conditions that the broader medical community has already begun to treat as neuroinflammatory targets. The pharmaceutical investment in galectin-3 inhibition for Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s is, in effect, also investing in the mechanistic case for IDA.

“The cascade does not care about diagnostic labels. It fires the same way in a fetal brain, a mid-life substantia nigra, a late-life cortex, and a young adult’s myelin sheath. The label is a downstream consequence of timing. The mechanism is upstream of all of them.”

Note on MS and TB006: TB006 has not yet entered clinical trials for multiple sclerosis. The galectin-3 evidence in MS is primarily preclinical (EAE mouse models and human lesion transcriptomics). The inclusion of MS in this framework is mechanistically supported but awaits clinical trial validation. This article will be updated when that evidence develops.
